I like science and speculative fiction as a cloudy mirror.
One where I come to regonise our present world and themes, but with enough initial distorition to be confronted with them as new.
Where you can't help but think about what it would be be like.

Lots of what I've read lately has been after the end-of-world, and this is another one.
But one that feels strangely hopeful.

The collapse here is catestrophic, more than most, an almost irrecoverabele loss of humanity.

Characters here old enough to remember talk about how they knew there was no untouched corner of the world left, because there were never any planes in the sky. Not long after reading this I looked up into the evening sky and saw the ever present orbit of contrails, before starry satilites began their shift.

You'd know it wasn't just your part of the world if all that went away one day and never came back.

The book covers transition between these worlds well.
Again I couldn't help but think of the present, these years of much smaller crises by contrast had a familliar feel of _one day I was worried about so many daily things, then suddenly many of them felt both irrelevant and small_.

In this newly emptied world, the book skipps the lame philosophy that states if we're stripped of our society life is nasty, brutish and short. It doesn't shy from the difficulties, but does a good job of makind you feel it's a continuation of human relations rather than a revelation of some long repressed deamons.

We follow characters who travel through this world as part of a symphony, making music and performing Shakespeare.
Entirely plausibly! For the last time the world was this small, weren't there also travelling bands of mummers doing the same?

But there's something bizzarely hopeful about it.
This isn't the description of people doing a bad job of re-enacting star wars to show what they'd lost.

This is saying that something, even art survives as a need.
Through a need to do more than survive.

Lovely book.
