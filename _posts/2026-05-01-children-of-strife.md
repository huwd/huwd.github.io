Aliens can be the worst bit of Sci-fi.

The idea of a rich and varied universe, that turns out to be empty is so dissapointing. I certainly feel a craving to know what's out there. Could I click my fingers and wish something into existance, a Wikipeida catalogue of all life in the Milky way, and serise of David Attenborough documentaries about them - would be high up the self gratifying part of the list.

I imagine a mix of the strange and familliar, surely there are things like fish, and things like birds, but in silicion base, not carbon. Surely they do things I could never imagine.

And that's the problem. There's a limit to what we can imagine for the truely alien, and more often it's just what's odd on earth, extrapolated into space.

My favourite thing about the "Children of" serise is that it leans into that dilemma hard. We don't _happen_ to have familiar body plans out in space, the aliens _are_ fundimentally familiar for good reasons.

With that small narriative licence we're then freed to look at just how truely alien life on the rest of our planet is. With all the dilligence and care a lifelong lover of zoology can muster.

The trope worked extremely well for the first two books, set among a epic and cinematic historical sweep that I found breathtaking and gripping. The third book resonated less with me, but only becuase it felt like a good episode of "prestige TV" where the others felt somehow like "cinema".

The tension every time a book is released is, how can Tchaikovsky re-present the central trope whilst avoiding repition, deviation or hesitation. By the third I feared the well was running dry.

Fortuantly, book four is a return to form, breaking genuinely new ground - whilst servicing it's increasing cast of previous appearances well. Moreover it felt properly cinematic again, a space opera of evolutionary scale.

A great read, I devoured it - I hope he writes more.
